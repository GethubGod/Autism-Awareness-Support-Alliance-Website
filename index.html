Step 1 · See the scale

Millions of autistic people, all over the world.

Autism is common and global. Estimates vary, but many experts suggest that roughly 1–2% of people are autistic. That’s tens of millions of individuals with their own experiences, needs, and strengths.

Step 2 · Hyperfocus

Feel a flow state — and how fragile it is.

This pattern game acts as a “hyperfocus zone.” If you get several rounds in a row correct, the noise cuts out and the site shifts into a calm flow state. If you break focus or navigate away, it snaps back.

How it works

Click the circles in order from smallest to largest. Get 5 rounds in a row correct to enter a Hyperfocus Flow State:

  • Visual noise and blur turn off.
  • A calm Lo-Fi track fades in.
  • The site feels fast, precise, and predictable.

Click a wrong circle or leave the section, and the site snaps back: the noise returns, blur comes back, and the flow is broken.

Step 3 · Everyday tasks under load

Simple tasks become hard when the environment fights you.

These examples use normal content — math, instructions, choosing lunch, filling a form, asking for help — and add sensory and social friction on top.

1. “Impossible” math problem

The math is elementary, but the numbers drift and a ticking sound speeds up as you try to read.

A train departs Station A at 8:00 AM traveling at 60 mph. Another train departs Station B at 9:00 AM traveling at 75 mph. If the stations are 300 miles apart, at what time will they pass each other?

2. Instruction paragraph “flashlight”

Only the area under your mouse is clear. The spacing creates distracting “rivers” down the text.

To assemble the device, insert the flange into the aperture. Ensure the bevel is facing upwards. Do not over-torque the primary screw, or the casing may shear. Once aligned, rotate the mechanism counter-clockwise until you feel a distinct click.

Quiet Room

A low-stimulus space. Soft colors, fewer elements, no pop-ups. While you stay here without interacting, your social battery steadily recharges.

In real life, breaks, alone time, and quiet rooms are not “avoiding” life — they are what make it possible to keep going.

Step 4 · Curb-cut reality

Accessibility is not just for autistic people — it’s for everyone.

When you switch on Accessibility mode, timers stop, noise stops, the form labels become clear, and reading gets easier. That’s the curb-cut effect: what helps disabled people tends to help almost everyone.

Listen first

Autistic people are the experts on autistic experience. Believing them is more effective than any simulator.

Change the environment

Instead of forcing people to “mask,” adjust noise, lighting, timing, and communication style.

Lower the cognitive load

Clear labels, predictable flows, and quiet spaces don’t “spoil” people — they let more people actually participate.
